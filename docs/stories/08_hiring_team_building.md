HIRING & TEAM BUILDING - DEPLOYMENT TEAM FORMATION AT DERQ

**Period**: 2023 onwards (starting ~1 year after joining Derq in August 2022)
**Role**: Hiring lead for the deployment engineering function (built from scratch)
**Scope**: Multiple hiring waves through three different channels (external agency, internal hiring manager, consulting agency)
**Outcome**: Built and grew a deployment engineering team from zero to the current configuration with two direct reports under guidance.

---

CONTEXT - WHY THE TEAM DIDN'T EXIST YET

I joined Derq in August 2022. At that point, the deployment team did not exist as a separate function. For roughly the first year, a colleague and I handled all deployment and maintenance work ourselves — software, ML, and engineering teams pitched in around our core development work.

Over that year, two things became clear. First, having experienced engineers spend their time on deployment and maintenance was not cost-effective: the work was important but the seniority needed for most of it was lower than what we were applying. Second, deployment quality was directly affecting team output — distractions from deployment incidents were degrading the throughput of the development engineers.

The pattern was unsustainable, but the fix was also the chance to do something thoughtful: build a deployment function deliberately, with the right people, the right onboarding, and the right interface to the rest of engineering.

WAVE 1 - FIRST HIRE THROUGH TOPHIRE

About a year after joining, we began forming the deployment team by hiring our first dedicated deployment engineer. I took a central role in driving the hire, working alongside HR with an external hiring agency called TopHire.

**My responsibilities through the wave:**

- **Initial screening framework.** Worked with TopHire to define the candidate guidelines and the skill set we were looking for. TopHire handled the first-pass screening using those guidelines.
- **Technical interviews.** Conducted technical interviews for the candidates TopHire shortlisted.
- **Assignment design.** For candidates who cleared the technical interview, I designed an assignment closely tied to the job description and the actual day-to-day work they would do if hired. This was specifically engineered to reveal *how* a candidate would contribute, not just whether they could pass an abstract test.
- **Final-round support.** Candidates who performed well on the assignment progressed to a final HR-cum-tech interview with the Engineering Manager. The EM was happy with both candidates I forwarded.
- **Final decision.** Given the EM's approval of both, the hiring call came back to me. I made the decision based on **team chemistry with the existing deployment engineer** — they were going to work together day in and day out, and I judged that the better working pairing would produce the better output, regardless of which candidate looked stronger on paper.
- **Contract negotiation.** Joined a call with TopHire and HR to walk through the offer — hiring terms, probation period, salary before and after probation. HR filled in gaps when I hit them; this was my first time leading a contract conversation, and they treated it as on-the-job training. The candidate accepted without pushback.

**What this wave taught me:** assignment design is the highest-leverage interview tool, because it reveals working style, not just knowledge. And team chemistry sometimes outweighs marginal differences in technical depth.

WAVE 2 - SECOND HIRE WITH INTERNAL HIRING MANAGER

A few months later, an increase in project volume created the need for more deployment engineers. By this point, Derq had brought on Gopal as an internal hiring manager — a meaningful shift from the agency-led model.

**My responsibilities through the wave:**

- **Sourcing.** Worked directly with Gopal to shortlist candidates who applied through LinkedIn and to proactively reach out to engineers whose profiles matched our JD.
- **Iterated screening questions.** Updated the screening question set based on what I'd learned from Wave 1. The improved questions translated into a much higher qualification rate — almost every candidate Gopal advanced cleared the next round.
- **Assignment review and filtering.** Used the assignment round to filter; this was where the real differentiation happened.
- **EM interview.** Same Engineering Manager final round as Wave 1.
- **Edge case: fresher candidate.** One of the final two qualified candidates was a fresher (no industry experience). To preserve team dynamics — the team's productivity depended on engineers carrying their own weight — I worked with HR to **modify our policies and explain additional terms** to the fresher, making the expectations and progression path explicit before the offer went out. This was the first time I'd had to design the policy around the person rather than fitting the person to existing policy.
- **Contract negotiation.** Gopal helped me through this round, but with a deliberate inversion — he let me lead the calls and gave feedback after each, rather than running them himself.

**What this wave taught me:** screening questions compound. Iterating them between hires is one of the highest-ROI activities in the entire hiring cycle. And new-hire policy isn't fixed — sometimes the right answer is to bend the policy thoughtfully rather than make a worse hire.

WAVE 3 - TWO MORE ENGINEERS VIA CONSULTING AGENCY

A few months later, additional project intake meant we needed two more deployment engineers. For this wave, I worked with a consulting agency rather than TopHire or in-house sourcing.

**Operationally:** the iterated screening questions from Waves 1 and 2 were reused. The consulting team did outreach to applicants I had shortlisted. The flow was tighter than the earlier waves, which is what compound improvements should look like.

**The salary-expectation challenge.** One of the candidates in this wave came in with salary expectations roughly **double our initial budget**. The reflexive options were to reject the candidate or to capitulate on budget. I did neither.

Instead, I engaged in **multiple rounds of conversation** with the candidate to:

1. **Provide market insights** — what comparable roles in the region actually paid, with data, not assertion.
2. **Highlight career growth specifics** — concrete progression paths within the team and the company, not generic "we have great culture" framing.
3. **Reframe the comp conversation around total trajectory** rather than year-one number.

We ultimately landed on a number that worked for both sides — a meaningful step down from the candidate's opening ask, a meaningful step up from our initial budget, and an offer the candidate accepted because the growth narrative was credible.

**What this wave taught me:** the gap between a candidate's ask and a company's budget is often closeable when both sides have full information — but it requires honesty about market data and concrete specificity about growth, not platitudes.

PROCESS DOCUMENTATION - PUSHING FOR LASTING INFRASTRUCTURE

Onboarding multiple engineers across different timelines surfaced a recurring inefficiency: each new hire was being onboarded somewhat ad hoc, with knowledge transfer happening in person and processes being explained verbally.

I advocated for **thorough documentation of our deployment and onboarding processes**. The upfront cost was real — writing docs took time during weeks when there was already too much to do — but the downstream payoff was larger: every subsequent onboarding was faster and put less load on the existing team. Documentation became infrastructure.

- Made the final hiring call on Wave 1 based on team chemistry, which proved out in practice.
- Negotiated a contract directly with a candidate whose salary expectations were ~2x our initial budget, landing a deal both sides accepted.
- Iterated screening questions between waves; qualification rate on advanced candidates rose materially by Wave 2.
- Designed policy adaptations for a fresher hire to preserve team dynamics without rejecting the candidate outright.
- Pushed for and produced **process documentation** that reduced onboarding burden on the existing team for every subsequent hire.

KEY LEARNINGS:

"Team chemistry isn't a soft factor — it's a direct multiplier on the team's output. Hiring the second-best candidate who fits beats hiring the best candidate who doesn't."

"Screening questions compound between waves. The iteration discipline matters more than the initial framework."

"When a candidate's salary ask is double your budget, neither rejecting them nor capitulating is the answer. Honest market data plus a specific growth narrative often closes the gap."

CONNECTION TO MBA GOALS:

The hiring journey at Derq is the clearest professional evidence of three things relevant to the MBA application:

1. **People leadership under no formal authority.** I had no "hiring manager" title during these waves — I owned the hiring because the team needed it owned, and the existing structure (HR, EM, eventually Gopal) was supportive rather than directive. MBA programs emphasize leadership without authority; this is what it looks like in practice.
2. **Process design and iteration.** Screening questions, assignment design, documentation push — all of these are systems-level interventions inside an unstructured process. The MBA's focus on operational excellence will give me the formal frameworks for the kinds of decisions I have been making intuitively.
3. **Negotiation under asymmetric information.** The 2x-budget candidate negotiation is the cleanest negotiation story I have. The MBA's negotiation curriculum (formal frameworks, simulations, structured practice) will let me approach negotiations of higher stakes with the same instinct but a much stronger toolkit.

This story also connects to the long-term goal of founding a company. Building a function from zero, hiring against ambiguous criteria, negotiating with limited budget, designing process where none existed — these are the exact things a founder does in the first two years. This work is rehearsal for that.
