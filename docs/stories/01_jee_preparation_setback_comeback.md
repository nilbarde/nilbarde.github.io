EARLY ACADEMIC FOUNDATION - RURAL INDIA TO IIT BOMBAY

DISCOVERING IIT & EARLY ACADEMIC EXCELLENCE

Growing up in a tiny village in rural India, I first learned about IIT during my 10th standard exams—a revelation that fundamentally changed my life's trajectory. Until that moment, I had no idea such institutions existed. This discovery inspired me to look beyond my village and pursue opportunities I hadn't known were possible.

My early academic journey was driven by necessity as much as ambition. Performing well in competitive examinations and earning scholarships was the only way to keep up with advantaged students from urban areas. I achieved my first notable success at age 10 by securing top rank in the state scholarship examination. Over the following years, I received merit-based scholarships for 8 out of my 12 years of pre-graduate education, which allowed me to complete my education independently without burdening my family.

Key early achievements included:
- State scholarship (age 10, and again in subsequent years)
- Maharashtra State Board three-level mathematics examinations (5th and 8th standards)
- National Means-cum-Merit Scholarship (NMMS) in 8th standard, which funded my school fees for the next 4 years
- Consistently ranked in the top 10 in Maharashtra and top 3 in my district across 10+ examinations in logic, critical thinking, and mathematics

MENTORS WHO CHANGED EVERYTHING

My school teachers, Sagar sir and Leena ma'am, went far beyond their professional duties. They stayed after school hours, invited me to their homes to teach new concepts, created personalized quizzes tailored to my learning needs, and even prepared my favorite meals during study sessions. They taught me to dream big, celebrated my wins, and motivated me through failures. I owe much of my success to their kindness and dedication. Years later, when I showed Sagar sir my IIT degree, he was emotionally overcome—seeing me at a world-class university had been his dream as much as mine.

JEE PREPARATION - MOVING TO PUNE AT AGE 15

Inspired by the IIT dream and encouraged by my teachers, I moved to Pune for JEE coaching at age 15—my first time away from home. The transition from a tiny village to this bustling educational hub was overwhelming. I had limited exposure to technology (internet access only through cyber cafes), limited knowledge of popular culture, and basic English skills from my village school.

Determined to find the best preparation, I researched coaching centers in Mumbai and Pune, reaching out to relatives in those cities for advice. After careful consideration, I chose Bakliwal Tutorials in Pune for its unique selection process. They conducted a half-day session where Bakliwal Sir led a lecture on logic and problem-solving techniques. My performance impressed him, and he encouraged me to join their institute. The coaching offered me a fee waiver scholarship for the second year based on my consistent performance in their internal examinations. I stayed in the top batch throughout the two years.

COLLABORATIVE LEARNING - FROM "ME VS ME" TO "WE"

Coming from a village where I had always been the top student, I initially felt intimidated in Pune by peers who had started their JEE preparation much earlier, attended schools with modified curricula for competitive exams, and had far better resources. It was a confidence-shattering experience at first.

Rather than getting discouraged, I approached my talented peers for help in subjects where I struggled, while sharing my mathematical problem-solving techniques with them in return. What started as a way to overcome my disadvantages became a philosophy I've carried forward throughout my life: learning is a two-way street that works best when everyone teaches and learns. Turning my initial hesitation and intimidation into a mutual growth opportunity taught me that my greatest asset isn't always what I know—it's my willingness to learn from others and help them in return.

THE SETBACK - 0.4% SHORT OF REQUIREMENT

When I finally took the JEE Advanced exam in my first attempt, I scored in the top 0.3% of 1.28 million applicants—well enough to secure admission to Aerospace Engineering at IIT Bombay, which was a good option given my interest in physics and mathematics. I had also cleared entrance exams for other top national institutes like BITS and VIT as backup options.

However, my IIT Bombay application was rejected due to falling just 0.4% short of the 75th percentile required in the 12th board exams. Devastated by this setback despite performing so well in JEE, I faced a critical decision: settle for BITS or VIT (both excellent institutions where I had secured admission), or bet on myself with a drop year.

THE DROP YEAR - STRATEGIC GAMBLE

Rather than settling for lesser alternatives, I took a calculated gamble on myself. I moved to a coaching institute in Mumbai, which offered me a fee waiver scholarship based on my JEE performance and proven track record. I dedicated the year to preparing for both JEE Advanced and 12th board exams simultaneously, ensuring I wouldn't fall short again.

The drop year was mentally challenging. While my peers moved on to college, I stayed behind, re-studying material and taking the same exams again. But the gamble paid off. The following year, I excelled in both exams and secured admission to the Engineering Physics branch at IIT Bombay—my dream branch and a perfect match for my academic interests.

CULTURAL ADAPTATION & GROWTH MINDSET

Throughout my journey from village to Pune to Mumbai, I encountered moments of casual discrimination and culture shock. Friends laughed when I shared my limited musical knowledge (only famous YouTube pop songs I'd discovered at cyber cafes). In my first year at IIT Bombay, my basic English skills and small vocabulary drew criticism.

Instead of feeling ashamed of my background, I chose to view these moments as learning opportunities. When peers criticized my English mistakes, I practiced more with those same people who had identified my limitations. When friends introduced me to new music genres, I explored them enthusiastically rather than hiding my initial ignorance. This pattern of converting criticism into improvement opportunities has served me well throughout my academic and professional journey.

I learned that as newcomers to any environment, we bear the responsibility to understand and integrate rather than expecting accommodation. Moving between diverse environments taught me to embrace unfamiliar customs as learning opportunities rather than threats to my identity.

REFLECTION:

The journey from a tiny village to IIT Bombay taught me lessons that transcend academics:

**On Scholarships**: What others might see as achievements, I experienced as survival strategies. Performing well wasn't optional—it was the only way to access opportunities available to my urban peers by default. This context shaped my work ethic and my commitment to giving back through mentorship.

**On Setbacks**: Falling 0.4% short after scoring in the top 0.3% of JEE could have broken me. Instead, it taught me to assess situations objectively: Was the gap closeable? Yes. Was I willing to invest a year? Yes. Was the dream worth the gamble? Absolutely. This framework—assessing gaps, evaluating options, making strategic bets—has guided every major career decision since.

**On Collaborative Learning**: Moving from a village where I competed only with myself to Pune where peers were as talented (or more talented) than me was humbling. The realization that I could learn from others while helping them with my strengths became a defining philosophy. Every mentor relationship, every team project, every professional collaboration since has been shaped by this two-way learning mindset.

**On Cultural Adaptation**: Being an outsider in Pune, then Mumbai, then Dubai has taught me that integration is my responsibility, not others' accommodation. This perspective—viewing unfamiliar customs as learning opportunities rather than threats—has enabled me to thrive across diverse environments.

**On Mentorship Debt**: Sagar sir and Leena ma'am invested in me when I was just a village student with potential. Their after-hours teaching, personalized attention, and emotional support created a debt I can never fully repay—only pay forward. Every junior I've mentored, every free lecture I've conducted, every scribe session I've volunteered for has been driven by this desire to be for someone else what they were for me.

**On Sunk Costs vs. Strategic Pivots**: When IIT rejected me despite my JEE score, many advised me to "move on" to BITS or VIT rather than "waste a year." But I distinguished between sunk costs (the preparation already done) and strategic pivots (investing one more year for the optimal outcome). The MBA decision follows the same logic: not settling for technical roles when business education can unlock greater impact.
